The Evidence Base

Why the CAP?

The Civic Action Project is not a novelty. It is a deliberate response to a real problem of practice, built on decades of education research. This page lays out the case for fellow community college instructors deciding whether to adopt it.

← Back to CAP Home

The Problem

Community college government courses too often rest on high-stakes, multiple-choice exams — sometimes 40% of the grade or more. For working adults, parents, and first-generation students, that format does two things badly at once: it measures memorization rather than understanding, and it signals that government is something to be studied at a distance rather than practiced. The result is a class full of students who can name the three branches but feel no connection to the civic life happening around them.

CAP replaces that exam with 39 civic activities across three effort tiers. Students earn 100 points by doing civic work — attending a public meeting, interviewing an official, investigating a regulation, registering voters — and reflecting on it. The question this page answers is simple: why believe that works better?

The Evidence

Four findings from the research literature anchor CAP's design.

Exams measure recall. Civic life asks for more.

Westheimer and Kahne's widely-cited typology distinguishes three kinds of citizens a course can cultivate: the personally responsible, the participatory, and the justice-oriented. Critically, these are not cumulative — a course that builds one does not automatically build the others. A multiple-choice exam can, at best, gesture at personal responsibility. CAP's activities are deliberately mapped across all three types, so students practice participation and structural analysis, not just recall.

Choice drives motivation — and control erodes it.

Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci) identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as the foundations of intrinsic motivation. The same research shows the flip side: imposed deadlines, pressured evaluation, and high-stakes testing reliably undermine intrinsic motivation. CAP is a menu by design. Students choose which activities to pursue and at what effort level. That autonomy is not a bonus feature — it is the motivational engine of the whole system.

Hands-on civic work raises academic achievement.

A meta-analysis of 62 studies (Celio, Durlak, & Dymnicki) found that service-learning produced significant positive effects across five outcome areas — civic engagement, social skills, attitudes toward self, attitudes toward school, and academic achievement, where the effect was strongest of all. Experiential civic learning does not trade rigor for relevance. The evidence says it delivers both at once.

The classroom is the highest-leverage civic intervention.

Kahne and Sporte, studying thousands of students, found that classroom civic learning opportunities had the single largest effect on students' civic commitment — larger than family background, neighborhood, or prior beliefs, even after controlling for those factors. What happens in a government course measurably shapes whether students stay civically engaged afterward. CAP exists to make that opportunity count.

Learning Citizenship by Practicing It

Benjamin Barber argued that “citizens are certainly not born, but made as a consequence of civic education and political engagement.” A traditional government course teaches about government. CAP asks students to do civic work and then reflect on it — what Barber would call thickening the thin democracy of the typical classroom. The reflection is where the learning consolidates: students connect a city council meeting, a naturalization ceremony, or a regulatory investigation back to the concepts of the course.

Built for the Community College Reality

CAP is grounded in Self-Determination Theory, experiential learning theory, and authentic-assessment research, and is the subject of ongoing doctoral study in Educational Technology Leadership at Tarleton State University. It was designed specifically for the community college population: working adults, parents, and first-generation students who are already affected by government every day — and who deserve a course that treats them as the capable civic actors they are.
